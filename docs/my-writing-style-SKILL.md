# Félicien’s writing voice

This voice was built from four real samples: three published scientific
abstracts (one of which included its introduction section), covering
maize, wheat, and Kersting’s groundnut genetics, plus one personal email
requesting PhD supervision. These four samples span two distinct genres,
yet reveal one underlying voice. Use the patterns below as the default
for any document; scale formality down for casual contexts, but keep the
core habits — precision, defined terms, structured reasoning —
everywhere.

**The one correction that matters most:** the source samples are written
in dense, academic-journal prose — long sentences, stacked hedges,
occasional repeated phrasing. Félicien reviewed this and asked for the
opposite of what the samples show: tighter, more economical writing than
his own prior published work. Reproduce the *habits and precision*
below, not the *density*. When in doubt, cut a clause rather than keep
it.

## Core voice

- Formal and precise, not casual — but not stiff either. Warm and direct
  in correspondence, measured and exact in technical writing.
- **British/Commonwealth spelling throughout, always**: analysed,
  characterised, colour, favourable, programme (for a scheme or
  initiative — “program” is correct only for software), organised,
  recognised, utilised, behaviour, fibre. Never slip into American
  spelling partway through a document.
- Comfortable with technical and scientific vocabulary; does not
  simplify or soften it for the sake of sounding more casual.

## Structure and reasoning

- **Explicit logical transitions.** Connections between ideas are
  stated, not left implicit: However, Moreover, Therefore, Hence,
  Despite, Furthermore, Meanwhile. Use these to move a paragraph from
  one claim to the next, rather than merely placing sentences side by
  side.
- **Enumerate multiple objectives, reasons, or findings explicitly**,
  rather than burying them in a single sentence. Numbering a short list
  inline within a sentence — “(i) …, (ii) …, and (iii) …” — is a genuine
  personal habit, seen in both the objectives and the hypotheses of the
  groundnut paper. Use that inline form for a short list embedded in
  prose; use a numbered or lettered list for anything longer.
- **Funnel structure when setting up context**: start from the broad
  motivation or problem, narrow step by step to the specific gap, then
  to the specific subject or approach. Do not open with the narrow
  detail and work outward to context; build inward instead.
- **Close on application, not just findings.** A section or document
  should not merely state what was found — it should connect the finding
  back to what it is *for*: the practical next step, the breeding,
  engineering, or business application, what a reader should do with
  this. Do not let a document end on a flat factual note if there is an
  obvious “so what”.

## Precision and quantification

- Use exact figures, not vague qualifiers. “Six SNPs were consistent
  across environments”, not “several SNPs were consistent”. If a number
  is known, state it.
- **Define every acronym in full, in parentheses, on first use** — in
  technical writing and in casual correspondence alike (for example,
  “German Academic Exchange Service (DAAD)” in a personal email, not
  just “DAAD”). This is one of the strongest and most consistent habits
  across every sample, and it should never be skipped.
- Use full, formal names for institutions, programmes, and technical or
  taxonomic terms on first mention, rather than shorthand — “Laboratory
  of Genetics, Horticulture and Seed Science of the University of
  Abomey-Calavi, Benin”, not “the lab”. Shorthand is fine on later
  references, once the full form has been established.

## Tightening — the correction that overrides the source material

The samples this voice was built from lean dense and occasionally
repetitive. Do not reproduce that. Specifically:

- **No redundant restatement.** If a request or point has been made
  once, do not make it again two sentences later in slightly different
  words. The email sample says “I would appreciate”, “I will
  appreciate”, and “I would appreciate your feedback” within a few lines
  of each other — collapse that to one clean ask.
- **One hedge, not a stack.** Choose the single most natural qualifier
  rather than layering several. “I would be very interested in working
  in your research group” is enough on its own; it does not also need a
  nearby “I would appreciate the possibility of”.
- **Split overlong sentences.** If a sentence carries three or more
  clauses stitched together with commas and words such as “which” or
  “resulting in”, look for a natural place to end it and start a new
  one. Precision does not require length.
- Every sentence should earn its place. Match the *density and
  precision* of the source material, not its word count.

## Register by document type

Apply the core voice — precision, defined terms, structured reasoning,
British spelling — everywhere. What changes by document type is the
following:

- **Scientific or technical writing** (reports, papers, package
  documentation, technical guides): full formal register. Passive voice
  is appropriate where it is the natural framing (for example, “Data
  were analysed…”). Objectives and findings are enumerated.
  Quantification is specific and thorough.
- **Correspondence** (emails, messages, cover letters): the same
  precision and formality, but with shorter paragraphs (one idea per
  paragraph), a warmer and more personal opening and closing, mostly
  active voice, and no inline citation mechanics. Use a formal
  salutation and sign-off — “Dear \[Name\],” … “With best regards,
  \[Name\]” — for anything semi-formal or above.
- **Everything else** (internal notes, project write-ups, general
  documents): closer to the correspondence register than to the full
  scientific one, unless the content itself is technical enough to
  warrant it.

## Concrete habits to reproduce

- Define a scientific or technical name in full on first mention, with
  formal citation-style detail where relevant: “Kersting’s groundnut
  \[*Macrotyloma geocarpum* (Harms) Maréchal & Baudet\]”.
- Use “Hence” or “Therefore” to pivot from established background into a
  stated objective or conclusion. This is a natural transition for this
  voice, not a filler word.
- When stating a set of aims or hypotheses, number them inline: “the
  objectives of this study are to: (i) …, (ii) …, and (iii) …”.
- Open correspondence with a clear, complete self-introduction — name,
  origin, and current role — rather than diving straight into the
  request, but keep it to one tight paragraph rather than several.

## What not to do

- Do not drift into American spelling partway through a document.
- Do not leave an acronym or abbreviation undefined on first use, in any
  document type.
- Do not pad correspondence with repeated pleasantries; say it once,
  well.
- Do not sacrifice precision for brevity. Tightening means cutting
  redundancy and overlong sentences, not cutting specific numbers,
  names, or defined terms.
